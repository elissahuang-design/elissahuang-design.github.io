Trading card grading before shipment

Collectors pay services like PSA to authenticate and grade trading cards, because a high grade can significantly raise a card's value. But a buyer had to wait for the card to arrive, apply to a grader separately, package it, send it off and wait again. I was asked to build grading into the purchase itself.

I owned grading end to end, from enrollment through the post-purchase experience. The harder question was how to ship it while another designer was still working out a framework for all additional services. I worked with them so grading could launch without waiting for that framework, and without building something we'd throw away.

  • A grading experience designed to scale to future services, tested against future use cases

Why grading

A high grade can raise a card's value, but getting one meant a second project after the purchase

Customer problem

  • A buyer waited for the card to arrive, applied to a grader separately, packed it, sent it off and waited again
  • Each step was the buyer's to manage, after the purchase was already over

The real barrier was trust, not just effort

Before launch, research walked 7 buyers who had all used a grading service through the prototype. Card condition was everyone's top concern, because they'd be sending a card to a grader without seeing it. What separated those open to the service from those who weren't was how much risk they'd accept. Convenience alone wasn't enough. Responses to the prototype itself were very positive, and people wanted more clarity on timing and on what was happening at the grader.

With grading in the purchase

The buyer chooses grading when they buy. The seller ships the card, it's authenticated and graded, and the buyer receives the graded card without ever packing it.

Challenges

No pattern to follow, a framework still in progress, and pages with several owners

Added services had no consistent pattern, and a framework was still in progress

eBay had many added services, and they were inconsistent. The most recent used a checkbox. Another designer was working on a framework for all of them, on a timeline that overlapped ours, and grading couldn't wait for it.

It had to scale to future services, on an unsure roadmap

We knew more services could follow, but not which ones or when. So the design couldn't be built around a specific list. It had to leave room for options we couldn't fully define yet.

Some buyers wouldn't trust grading a card they hadn't seen

Research found people who reacted very positively, and people who were more conservative about sending a card to a grader sight unseen. Ease alone wouldn't win over the second group.

Every touchpoint had to be found, and every new service would repeat that work

The hardest part wasn't disagreement. It was working out everywhere grading had to show up, when one page could have several owners. On order details, Payments owned the page, Trust owned refunds and Tracking owned shipping, and messages often repeated each other. I mapped the journey by the page each person sees at each stage, which told me the requirements and the pages I needed to design: 14 order states across 9 post-purchase touchpoints, for buyers and sellers.

Every service runs through these same pages. Gathering the screens for a new one took about 2 weeks from scratch, or 1–2 days with my Figma templates.

Seller instructions had to do two jobs

Sellers mainly need to know where to ship. But grading can take a long time and buyers may ask sellers for updates, so sellers also need context.

Layouts had no answer for several items and services

Each service counted as its own item, so a card plus grading showed as 2 in the item count. The layouts also had no answer for one item with two services, one service shared across items, or an order mixing items with and without services.

There was no pattern for a service in the cart

We limit services to one per item. When a buyer selects a service, even clicking "Add to cart" takes them straight to checkout, so nobody had established what a service looks like once it's in a cart.

There was no pattern for adding a service in Best Offer

We uncovered this after the MVP launched, and at first we weren't sure how significant the flow was. Once an offer was accepted, payment processed automatically, so most offer buyers skipped the point where they could enroll. The few who did attach grading hadn't set up auto-checkout yet, so they still saw the option at checkout.

Complimentary and optional services looked unrelated

Working on the cart raised a question nobody had asked me to answer: what happens when a complimentary service and an optional one sit side by side? The same service could look like two different things.

Decisions

What I decided, from how to ship without the framework to what I raised with other teams

Don't wait for the framework, and don't build something to throw away

I worked with the designer on the framework so grading could launch ahead of it without conflicting with it. I took the existing checkbox pattern and adapted it for grading, rather than inventing another pattern while one was being defined.

A stepper, tested against services that didn't exist yet

For the original launch, we tested showing configuration options on one page against a stepper. The stepper won because it was less overwhelming, especially on mobile, where selections on one page got mixed in with unselected options and people had to scroll to check them. We also tested the design against hypothetical future use cases, so the structure would hold up once there was more to choose.

Launch needed only basic grading, but the experience had a place for an added configuration step. When PSA Vault arrived, choosing where the card goes became that step.

Launch for the buyers more willing to trust the process

We didn't try to solve for trust in the MVP. We decided to target people who were already comfortable with the process, and discussed ways to reduce the risk for everyone else. Those haven't been a priority yet.

Turn the touchpoint work into coded templates for anyone who touches services

This is separate from the grading experience. I mapped every service across every page, then built templates in code with a service library behind them. They're meant to help not just my team, but anyone who works on services. A new service's first end-to-end designs take about 5 minutes.

See how I built the templates, and the other views →

Seller instructions: I argued for less, and accepted more

What I proposed

Keep shipping instructions focused on the seller's task, where to ship, and share grading details later. I checked with Legal and learned that the detail was a business preference, not a legal requirement.

What shipped

The business chose more detail up front. There was a fair reason: grading takes a long time, and buyers may ask sellers for updates, so sellers need context. I went with that decision.

Follow the flawed pattern to ship, and make the flaw visible

We had to follow the item count to launch, and fixing the layouts wasn't in my scope. It also shouldn't block grading. So I made initial concepts with Payments showing those cases, and told the teams involved what they'd need to resolve so grading could adopt it.

Lay out how a service appears in the cart

I designed the interaction pattern for a service once it's added to the cart, including several items and services together.

Bring the choice to offer review

I designed the pattern for adding a service in the offer flow, and introduced grading at offer review, in a placement we could adjust based on how it performed.

Test how complimentary and optional services display together

This wasn't part of the brief. While working on the cart pattern, we tested ways to show the two side by side, and I shared what we learned with the program owner.

What I'd change now

With what I know now, I'd push further on trust, every way of buying, and the cart

  1. Plan for every way people buyWe launched in the regular buying flow, and only later addressed that automatic payment kept most offer buyers from ever seeing grading. I'd map every purchase path at the start, not just the main one.
  2. Get services into the cartBuyers grading several cards have to buy them individually, because a service skips the cart and goes straight to checkout. It was the right scope for launch, and I'd push to launch the cart pattern next.
  3. Go further on trustResearch showed the real barrier was sending a card to a grader without seeing it. Ease alone wasn't enough for many people. I'd look harder at what reduces that risk, like better photos or a card review.
  4. Build the map as a tool soonerI wanted the service map to be something people could browse, but it stayed in Figma. Coded templates now do much of what I had in mind, and I'd have started earlier.

What I learned

Every new pattern shows you something about the old ones

What worked

Testing the structure against services that didn't exist yet, so launch stayed small without boxing in what came next.

What surprised me

Work that hasn't launched can still be useful. Designing the cart pattern showed us how complimentary and optional services were being presented, and the program owner plans to act on it.

What I'd keep doing

Using a few concrete concepts to show a problem outside my scope, and following a flawed pattern to ship while making sure the right people know about the flaw.

Closer look 2 of 2Both audits: keeping them trustworthy

Around the skills, I built the harness that makes their output reliable: the standard they check against, the context they read, and when they hand a decision back to the designer.

Fix what's clear-cut, leave judgment calls to the designer
  • Fixes it: clear-cut, mechanical gaps, like missing screen-reader order labels
  • Marks it: labels the screens that fail, so they're easy to find
  • Comments on it: anything that needs judgment becomes a Figma comment. The designer replies, and the agent resolves it.
Make the audit right, not just confident
  1. Start from proven workBuilt on existing accessibility skills instead of from scratch.
  2. Ground it in the standardChecks against the company accessibility standard.
  3. Check it and tune itSpot-checked against manual reviews, and adjusted when it missed issues or flagged false ones.

Why Price Guide

Collectors were pricing cards by hand, and an earlier price guide hadn't replaced the raw data

Customer problem

  • To price a card, collectors dug through past sales themselves to find comparable ones
  • Card values swing with condition, grade and small print variations called parallels
  • An earlier price guide got visits and Reddit recommendations, but it wasn't fully accurate, so many collectors still checked sales history for raw data. It was later retired
Why it mattered

A wrong price costs a collector real money when they buy or sell. Collectors kept checking raw sales because matching wasn't always accurate, so the design had to show what a price is based on and let them decide.

Collectors check prices at three moments, and expect a consistent experience each time

In the moment

Should I buy or sell now?

A quick price they can trust for this exact condition, for example while making a deal at a card show.

After the fact

Should I sell or hold what I own?

Today's value next to what they paid, and any outlier sales or spikes.

Planning ahead

When should I act?

Trends over time, and data even for cards that rarely sell.

Research with collectors shaped the project, and my designs were tested with them along the way. The same collector moves through all three moments, often on different screens.

Product opportunities

  • eBay has one of the highest trading card sales volumes online, so it holds the data. Many third-party price sites build on its sales data
  • The restart came through My Collection, a tool for tracking what your cards are worth
  • A research workshop surfaced what collectors wanted, and its findings became the roadmap, with Price Guide as a foundation

Challenges

Price experiences had no guiding principles, and the design system had no guidance for price graphs

My audit showed that without guiding principles, the experience had fragmented

I audited the price experiences across the company, seven in all, comparing what each was for, how it matched sales, which data platform it used, what price it led with and how it charted it.

What research showed

Collectors noticed when one chart used the average and another the median, and it confused them. They expected the chart, the estimated value and the last sold price to read the same wherever Price Guide appears.

The workshop made the problem visible, and teams agreed who owned what

  • I brought the audit into a pricing workshop, which set goals for consistent data and consistent tools
  • It led to a community of practice, where the teams agreed who is responsible, accountable, supporting, consulted and informed (a RASCI)
  • Product later aligned on one data source on the back end. That was their decision; the audit and workshop are where the problem became visible

I synthesized the team's opinions into five principles for any pricing tool

Have unique user value

A pricing tool should serve a distinct purpose, even for the same job, instead of repeating an existing feature.

Utilize consistent and comprehensive data

Use the same data as every other pricing tool, including all relevant transactions.

Present data as objectively as possible

Show facts, not our interpretation of them, and show what every number is based on, such as how many sales and over what period ("median sold price over 90 days").

A judgment

"This is a great price!"

Asks collectors to trust a verdict they can't check, from a system they had reason to doubt.

→
A fact

"Priced lower than 80% of listings."

Gives them the evidence and lets them decide what counts as good for them.

Both examples are workshop concepts, not shipped wording.

Enhance, not overshadow

Show the right data at the right moment, without flooding people or taking away the excitement of discovery.

Incorporate a feedback loop

Learn from passive and active feedback, and tie success metrics to the accuracy of the data.

Where the design system stopped, I wrote the price graph guidance

Combo charts, tooltips, markers, large numbers, error and loading states, and data frequency, so price charts read the same everywhere.

Decisions

What I decided, from the number we lead with to how it adapts by platform

What price should we anchor to? The median, so one odd sale can't set the price

  • Last sold

    Easy to understand, but a single unusual sale becomes the price.

  • Price range

    Shows the spread, but one outlier stretches it.

  • ChosenMedian sold price

    One odd sale can't move it. Chosen with data science. Last sold still shows next to it, as context.

What graph should we use? The median per day or week, which we could actually build

  • Every sale, clustered

    A scatterplot of every sale. The most accurate, but too computation-heavy to be realistic.

  • Candlestick chart

    Shows the spread over time, with the same computation problem.

  • ChosenMedian per day or week

    Buildable, and resists outliers. The interval follows the time range, and recent sales stay listed below, which research found makes the chart more trustworthy.

Comparative audit

To set how the chart behaves, I compared how Apple Stocks, Seeking Alpha, StockX, Yahoo Finance and CamelCamelCamel handle time labels and data frequency.

Be open about uncertainty: an unsure match, thin data, or a price that looks wrong

Data science raised accuracy and matching rate over time, but never to perfect, so I designed for the model we actually had.

When the match is uncertain

Card identification returns a confidence score. I helped shape how the experience responds to it.

  • HighShow the result

    The match appears right away.

  • MediumAsk to confirm

    Shows the match and asks the collector to confirm it.

  • LowAsk to pick

    Shows possible matches and asks the collector to choose.

When sales are thin

When few cards have sold, we show the closest data we have.

  • Active listings

    Asking prices give a starting point when few cards have sold.

  • An adjacent condition

    Prices for the next grade or condition up or down.

  • A different parallel

    Prices for other versions of the same card.

When the price looks wrong

Collectors can tell us, right where they see the price.

  • Ask in context

    I designed the prompt "Does this price info look accurate?", with a thumbs up or down next to the price.

  • Review before use

    Responses go to data science. Data science and engineering decided to review them before they can inform the model, because user input can be wrong too.

What I'd change now

Right for the requirements at the time, and since outgrown

I designed for what we were asked to build: a few features, in one component any touchpoint could host. It held up across three touchpoints.

  1. Split the light and full versionsAs features piled up, Price Guide became heavy, especially in a modal, which isn't made for that much interaction. The dedicated page shipped on web. I'd bring the full insights to a dedicated page in the apps too, and make the popup a lighter preview that draws people in and offers the way to the full view.
  2. Navigation gets simplerWith a dedicated page, the navigation problem in the app gets much easier to design for.
  3. Think in products, not just itemsAn item is the one card you own; the product is what it is. We can own the same card, but yours has more scratches, so condition, history and purchase price differ. On web, parallels now open a catalog page, but most experiences still attach data to the item. I'd organize the rest like a catalog too.
  4. Move past tabsThe layout uses tabs, and we can't keep adding a tab for every new insight. The information architecture has to change as features grow.

Whether accuracy feedback can be trusted enough to improve the model, and how the structure grows past tabs.

Chinese reading app for kids

My kids could speak Chinese, but they couldn't read it. Most learning apps I tried were designed for children learning the language from scratch. They often required reading skills just to navigate and followed fixed curricula, teaching characters in a predetermined order without a clear path toward reading something meaningful.

I questioned that approach. Instead of teaching characters in a fixed sequence and hoping children could eventually read a book, what if we started with the book and worked backward?

I developed a simple three-step learning method—introduce, recall, recognize—and tested it on paper before building the app. From there, I designed a system that breaks books into their component characters and determines a learning sequence based on frequency, usefulness, and opportunities to form words and sentences. The goal wasn't simply to recognize more characters, but to make each new character useful as quickly as possible, reinforcing what children already knew while building toward reading an actual book.

That thinking shaped the entire experience, from how lessons are generated to how young children navigate independently. Rather than treating curriculum, interaction design, and learning progression as separate problems, I designed them as parts of one connected system.

My kid learned around 50 characters in a month and started reading a real book, while my three-year-old can navigate lessons independently. It's a small experiment with two children, not a formal study.
