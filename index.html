Trading Card Grading

Growing grading into a service others could build on

In March 2025, we launched grading as part of the eBay purchase. Grading lets collectors have a card’s condition professionally assessed and scored—a high grade can significantly increase its value. Buyers could now choose that service during purchase, with their card moving through authentication and grading before reaching them. I led the end-to-end experience, bringing 11 teams together across the buyer and seller journey.

The launch was the beginning of a longer workstream. As we expanded the service, I kept returning to the same touchpoints and encountering questions that extended beyond grading. Other services needed to appear in those places too, but the existing patterns didn’t fully account for how they could work together.

That became a question I carried through the work: how could I use what we were learning from grading to improve services more broadly, while keeping each launch moving?

The starting point

What did the first launch change?

Previously, the buyer received the card and arranged grading separately. Bringing that choice into the purchase connected enrollment, seller instructions, authentication, grading, tracking, and delivery in one experience.

Research also showed the limit of convenience: some buyers were uncomfortable sending a card for grading before inspecting it themselves. For the MVP, we focused on buyers willing to trust that process. Reducing the risk for more cautious buyers remained further work.

Challenges

What made that growth difficult?

Existing patterns didn’t cover what was coming next.

At the start, there were examples of how services appeared in the purchase, but no established framework. As I designed grading and explored future combinations of items and services, I found cases those patterns didn’t address. We needed a way forward for grading while making those gaps visible for the broader service work.

The experience needed room to grow.

The MVP offered basic grading, but enrollment needed to accommodate additional choices over time. I had to keep the first experience straightforward without building a structure that would become limiting as the service expanded.

There was no shared reference for the full experience.

Each service had its own documentation. Finding the relevant designs was difficult, and for some longstanding services, the documentation was outdated or missing. Before I could understand how grading should fit, I had to piece together how services already worked across the buyer and seller journey.

Foundations

What foundations did I put in place?

A shared view of how services work

Each service had its own documentation, and some of it was outdated or missing. I brought the existing experiences into a shared reference showing how services appeared across the buyer and seller journey.

The documentation supported more than grading. Other teams used it to align their service experiences, and it helped inform the development of the broader service framework.

For grading, mapping 14 order states across 9 post-purchase touchpoints made the scope concrete. The buyer and seller maps below show where I designed for new states and where the experience used existing patterns.

Explore the buyer and seller journey maps

Enrollment with room for another choice

For the original launch, we tested configuration on a single page against a stepped flow. The stepped version was less overwhelming, especially on mobile, where selected and unselected options became difficult to review on one page.

I also tested the structure against hypothetical configurations. The item’s existing state changed which services were relevant, so the flow needed to adapt the available choices as well as the steps.

Outcome · PSA Vault

When PSA Vault was introduced, choosing whether to send the graded card to storage or to the buyer fit the additional step in the enrollment structure.

Expanding the experience

How did grading reach buyers using offers?

After the MVP, we found grading attachment in offer-based purchases was far below the regular flow. Once an offer was accepted, payment processed automatically for most buyers, skipping the checkout step where they could choose grading.

I designed a pattern for adding a service in the offer flow, bringing grading into offer review before payment. Buyers could also select grading on the item page before making an offer.

Outcome · Offers

Grading attachment in the offer flow tripled after the extension. The result showed why expansion also meant considering different ways people buy, not only adding service options.

Beyond grading

How did I help close the gaps beyond grading?

Bringing missing cases into the framework work

I explored combinations of items and services that the existing patterns couldn’t accommodate and shared those cases with partner teams. My grading work and these explorations helped establish the need for a dedicated service-framework workstream, with a designer assigned to develop it.

A card with grading counted as two items in the existing layout. I worked with Payments on initial concepts for more complex relationships, including one item with multiple services and orders mixing items with and without services. Grading could launch within the existing experience while these cases informed the broader framework work.

Designing the service-in-cart pattern

I designed how services could appear in the cart, including orders containing multiple items and services. The work also surfaced a related problem: complimentary and paid versions of a service looked unrelated.

Testing showed that bringing their visual treatment closer helped people recognize them as the same type of service, while keeping the free option clear. I shared those findings with the program owner to inform their service presentation.

The cart pattern has not launched; the research findings informed further work in another program.

Building on the reference

How could this work make the next service easier?

The shared reference also became a starting point for a separate tool-building effort. I built coded journey templates and a service library so I could carry the existing structure into new service designs.

The templates are in my own use; they are intended for others who work on services. The documentation’s cross-team use and the templates’ potential reach are different stages of this work.

Learnings and next steps

What did I learn, and what would I improve next?

What I’m proudest of is the reach beyond the immediate launch. Work on one service gave me a way to improve the shared guidance and bring unresolved cases into a broader framework effort, while continuing to deliver grading.

AI Project Assistant

Making project decisions easier to find and follow

Every project kept coming back to the same four questions: what was decided, who decided it, why, and what's still open. The answers existed, but they were spread across Slack, meetings, email and docs, so I'd ask around and piece the timeline together by hand.

When Claude could reach our internal tools, I built skills for my own workflow. They pull context from Slack, Zoom, Outlook and Google Drive into the design brief format the team already reads, separate confirmed decisions from open ones, flag related work, and link back to sources so people can check them.

The problem

Why was finding a decision so much work?

1 · What we needed to know
  1. What was decided?
  2. Who decided it?
  3. Why?
  4. What's still open?
2 · Where the answers lived
  • SlackThreads and quick calls
  • ZoomMeeting discussions
  • OutlookApprovals and follow-ups
  • Google DriveRequirements and briefs

Finding an answer meant searching across tools and checking with teammates to piece together what had been decided.

3 · Where it broke down

Example: how one decision traveled across 10+ stakeholders

  1. Leadership feedback conflicts with an existing guideline
  2. The team checks with core members and legal for the history, across several threads
  3. Marketing shares a point of view that conflicts with the core team's discussion
  4. The decision is escalated to leadership
  5. Teammates outside some threads lose track of the latest context
  6. Once decided, it takes more threads to find the right owners and share it

Requirements

What would make this useful to the whole team?

With a limited AI budget, I was the one running the assistant. Most teammates didn’t have an agent set up, so the output needed to be useful on its own.

What the output needed to do

Readable without AI

A finished document, not something you have to query to understand.

In a format the team knows

The existing design-brief template, already expected at kickoff and handoff, so people know where to look.

Easy to correct

A doc anyone can edit, so the team can fix or add context the AI missed.

Why I chose a shared document

I chose an editable Google Doc in the team’s existing design-brief format, so teammates could find answers, correct mistakes, and add context without an agent of their own.

Efficiency and reuse

How could the team stay informed without repeating the work?

I chose scheduled updates to a shared brief so the team could reuse the same summary instead of generating it for every question. The tradeoff was freshness: the brief could fall behind between updates.

$150
Monthly AI usage creditBilled by use, so every retrieval and summary drew on a limited budget.

Review workflow

What does the assistant do, and what do I control?

The assistant gathers the project history. In my setup, it doesn’t write into the shared document on its own: I review its findings privately, fill in missing context, and tell it what to update.

  1. Step 1Gather the context

    The assistant searches project conversations and documents, then lists confirmed decisions, pending items, and questions.

  2. Step 2 · My roleReview and clarify

    I check the findings and answer its questions. Sometimes I add context from a conversation or follow-up it couldn’t access.

  3. Step 3 · My roleChoose what to update

    Once the decisions are clear, I tell the assistant which section of the design brief to update.

  4. Step 4Write into the shared brief

    The assistant applies the update to the specified section, where teammates can read and edit it.

The assistant separates explicit decisions from pending discussions. When sources conflict or information is missing, it asks me to clarify rather than treating the summary as complete.

Testing also showed me that an updated brief can still look out of date: one contained October decisions but displayed an August update date. Checking the document’s freshness remains part of my review.

Sources it can search: Slack, Zoom, Outlook, Google Drive, Figma, company search and the internal research library.

Closer look 2 of 2Both audits: keeping them trustworthy

Around the skills, I built the harness that makes their output reliable: the standard they check against, the context they read, and when they hand a decision back to the designer.

Fix what's clear-cut, leave judgment calls to the designer
  • Fixes it: clear-cut, mechanical gaps, like missing screen-reader order labels
  • Marks it: labels the screens that fail, so they're easy to find
  • Comments on it: anything that needs judgment becomes a Figma comment. The designer replies, and the agent resolves it.
Make the audit right, not just confident
  1. Start from proven workBuilt on existing accessibility skills instead of from scratch.
  2. Ground it in the standardChecks against the company accessibility standard.
  3. Check it and tune itSpot-checked against manual reviews, and adjusted when it missed issues or flagged false ones.
  4. Experts keep the final sayFewer issues need escalating; the accessibility team still reviews what matters.

  • Alignment: the variants page shows where older services can share one framework

What I learned

What I learned from building on a budget

What worked

Matching the tool to the constraints of the moment: plugins when AI couldn't reach our tools, skills while handoff stayed in Figma, code once AI could use the design system.

Spend credits on things that get reused: a plugin, a skill or a template costs once and pays back every time. Setup is still too hard for most designers, and the numbers are estimates from early use.

What's next · Without the budget limit

Today I start every task myself and watch it run, because each run costs credits. With more budget, I'd set up automation and delegation.

    Define the outcome and what "done" looks like before AI starts.

    The preflight checks become the definition of a ready handoff file, and the service library defines what every new service needs.

Price Guide

Helping collectors make sense of what a card is worth

A trading card's value moves with recent sales, condition and small print variations called parallels. Collectors had to dig through sold listings themselves or use third-party sites built on eBay's data. And every price we showed would sit on two models, one identifying the card and one matching sales, and neither is always right.

A research workshop surfaced what collectors wanted, and its findings became the roadmap, with Price Guide as a foundation. I designed it as one component rather than a page, so card scanning, the item page and My Collection could all use it.

What I owned

  • The experience architecture: Price Guide as one component that any touchpoint can use
  • The features built on it: comparing across grades and conditions, and comparing across parallels
  • Patterns that work across desktop web, mobile web and the apps
  • An audit of seven price experiences, and a workshop to share it and set principles for how prices are presented

Why Price Guide

Collectors were pricing cards by hand, and an earlier price guide hadn't replaced the raw data

Customer problem

  • To price a card, collectors dug through past sales themselves to find comparable ones
  • Card values swing with condition, grade and small print variations called parallels
  • An earlier price guide got visits and Reddit recommendations, but it wasn't fully accurate, so many collectors still checked sales history for raw data. It was later retired
Why it mattered

A wrong price costs a collector real money when they buy or sell. Collectors kept checking raw sales because matching wasn't always accurate, so the design had to show what a price is based on and let them decide.

Collectors check prices at three moments, and expect a consistent experience each time

In the moment

Should I buy or sell now?

A quick price they can trust for this exact condition, for example while making a deal at a card show.

After the fact

Should I sell or hold what I own?

Today's value next to what they paid, and any outlier sales or spikes.

Planning ahead

When should I act?

Trends over time, and data even for cards that rarely sell.

Research with collectors shaped the project, and my designs were tested with them along the way. The same collector moves through all three moments, often on different screens.

Product opportunities

  • eBay has one of the highest trading card sales volumes online, so it holds the data. Many third-party price sites build on its sales data
  • The restart came through My Collection, a tool for tracking what your cards are worth
  • A research workshop surfaced what collectors wanted, and its findings became the roadmap, with Price Guide as a foundation

Challenges

Price experiences had no guiding principles, and the design system had no guidance for price graphs

My audit showed that without guiding principles, the experience had fragmented

I audited the price experiences across the company, seven in all, comparing what each was for, how it matched sales, which data platform it used, what price it led with and how it charted it.

The same card, in two tools

What research showed

Collectors noticed when one chart used the average and another the median, and it confused them. They expected the chart, the estimated value and the last sold price to read the same wherever Price Guide appears.

The workshop made the problem visible, and teams agreed who owned what

  • I brought the audit into a pricing workshop, which set goals for consistent data and consistent tools
  • It led to a community of practice, where the teams agreed who is responsible, accountable, supporting, consulted and informed (a RASCI)
  • Product later aligned on one data source on the back end. That was their decision; the audit and workshop are where the problem became visible

I synthesized the team's opinions into five principles for any pricing tool

Have unique user value

A pricing tool should serve a distinct purpose, even for the same job, instead of repeating an existing feature.

Utilize consistent and comprehensive data

Use the same data as every other pricing tool, including all relevant transactions.

Present data as objectively as possible

Show facts, not our interpretation of them, and show what every number is based on, such as how many sales and over what period ("median sold price over 90 days").

A judgment

"This is a great price!"

Asks collectors to trust a verdict they can't check, from a system they had reason to doubt.

→
A fact

"Priced lower than 80% of listings."

Gives them the evidence and lets them decide what counts as good for them.

Both examples are workshop concepts, not shipped wording.

Enhance, not overshadow

Show the right data at the right moment, without flooding people or taking away the excitement of discovery.

Incorporate a feedback loop

Learn from passive and active feedback, and tie success metrics to the accuracy of the data.

Where the design system stopped, I wrote the price graph guidance

Combo charts, tooltips, markers, large numbers, error and loading states, and data frequency, so price charts read the same everywhere.

Decisions

What I decided, from the number we lead with to how it adapts by platform

What price should we anchor to? The median, so one odd sale can't set the price

  • Last sold

    Easy to understand, but a single unusual sale becomes the price.

  • Price range

    Shows the spread, but one outlier stretches it.

  • ChosenMedian sold price

    One odd sale can't move it. I recommended it, and my PM agreed it with data science. Last sold still shows next to it, as context.

What graph should we use? The median per day or week, which we could actually build

  • Every sale, clustered

    A scatterplot of every sale. The most accurate, but too computation-heavy to be realistic.

  • Candlestick chart

    Shows the spread over time, with the same computation problem.

  • ChosenMedian per day or week

    Buildable, and resists outliers. The interval follows the time range, and recent sales stay listed below, which research found makes the chart more trustworthy.

Comparative audit

To set how the chart behaves, I compared how Apple Stocks, Seeking Alpha, StockX, Yahoo Finance and CamelCamelCamel handle time labels and data frequency.

Be open about uncertainty: an unsure match, thin data, or a price that looks wrong

Data science raised accuracy and matching rate over time, but never to perfect, so I designed for the model we actually had.

When the match is uncertain

Card identification returns a confidence score. I helped shape how the experience responds to it.

  • HighShow the result

    The match appears right away.

  • MediumAsk to confirm

    Shows the match and asks the collector to confirm it.

  • LowAsk to pick

    Shows possible matches and asks the collector to choose.

When sales are thin

When few cards have sold, we show the closest data we have.

  • Active listings

    Asking prices give a starting point when few cards have sold.

  • An adjacent condition

    Prices for the next grade or condition up or down.

  • A different parallel

    Prices for other versions of the same card.

When the price looks wrong

Collectors can tell us, right where they see the price.

  • Ask in context

    I designed the prompt "Does this price info look accurate?", with a thumbs up or down next to the price.

  • Review before use

    Responses go to data science. Data science and engineering decided to review them before they can inform the model, because user input can be wrong too.

Built to scale: one component, so Price Guide could show up wherever collectors need it

I designed Price Guide as a component that any touchpoint can host, instead of a one-off page. That's what let it spread beyond its first home.

Adjust for platform: as the feature scaled, I identified the need for a dedicated page

A dedicated page would help people navigate, and reduce the information load on the modal. Collectors jump between grades and parallels, and that gets deep fast.

Desktop web

Exploring a grade or parallel can open in a new tab. The original listing stays where it was.

What I'd change now

Right for the requirements at the time, and since outgrown

I designed for what we were asked to build: a few features, in one component any touchpoint could host. It held up across three touchpoints.

  1. Split the light and full versionsAs features piled up, Price Guide became heavy, especially in a modal, which isn't made for that much interaction. The dedicated page shipped on web. I'd bring the full insights to a dedicated page in the apps too, and make the popup a lighter preview that draws people in and offers the way to the full view.
  2. Navigation gets simplerWith a dedicated page, the navigation problem in the app gets much easier to design for.
  3. Think in products, not just itemsAn item is the one card you own; the product is what it is. We can own the same card, but yours has more scratches, so condition, history and purchase price differ. On web, parallels now open a catalog page, but most experiences still attach data to the item. I'd organize the rest like a catalog too.
  4. Move past tabsThe layout uses tabs, and we can't keep adding a tab for every new insight. The information architecture has to change as features grow.

What I learned

I couldn't wait for a perfect model, so I designed for the one we had

What worked

Being honest about uncertainty: facts over verdicts, a median that resists outliers, and states that change with confidence.

What I'd keep doing

Designing the foundation as a component early, so new touchpoints get the same experience without a redesign.

What's still open

Whether accuracy feedback can be trusted enough to improve the model, and how the structure grows past tabs.

Chinese Reading App

Helping my kids read the Chinese books they already love

My kids speak Chinese, but they couldn't read it, and the apps and materials I tried didn't fit them. So I started from a book we already own, called Mama, and worked backward to the characters a child needs to read it.

Problem

Nothing I tried fit how we read at home

Built for non-speakers

Many apps teach Chinese from zero. My kids already speak it; what they need is to read it.

Too much to navigate

Apps for kids who already speak it are full of menus and extra features. A child who can’t read yet can’t get around on their own.

One script only

Some teach only simplified characters. I read traditional.

A fixed order

A set curriculum teaches characters in its own order. If a character isn’t in the book we’re reading, my kids can’t use it outside the lesson.

Competitive analysis

What’s already out there

I tried Hongen and Sagebooks, both built for kids who already speak Chinese; the others are from reviews. Sagebooks came closest and partly inspired the app, but its order is fixed: one of the first characters is 山, mountain. When I read to my kids, I ask them to find the character they’re learning. If the book isn’t about mountains, there’s nothing to find, so the lesson stays in the lesson.

Approach

The book is the curriculum

What to teach

Any book we have on hand can become the curriculum. The app breaks it into the characters it uses, so each new character is one my kids will meet on the page, and what they learn turns into words and sentences they can read. It supports both traditional and simplified characters, and both pinyin and zhuyin.

How to teach it

I borrowed the method from how I taught my kids body parts when they were babies, and tested it on paper flash cards before writing any code. It worked, so the app is built around it.

In what order

I order the characters by combining three things. To find the best mix, I built a tool that runs the same book through different settings side by side and scores how soon each new character comes back in a word my kids can read. Over time, my kids have recognized more and more characters.

Use the algorithm, not AI, for the order

The lesson builder takes photos of a book’s pages and uses OCR to extract the text. I compared my ordering algorithm against Claude, and the results were about the same quality, so I went with the algorithm. Parents can then edit the lesson. For now this lives in a separate web tool; I plan to bring it into the app.

Decisions

Design decisions, and why I made them

For kids who can’t read yet

Use pictures over words

Graphics and icons wherever possible, so there’s little to read.

Make the next step obvious

Each screen makes clear what to do, so no instructions are needed.

Make touch targets bigger than necessary

Small fingers miss. The answer tiles are large, and when kids drag a sound onto a character, the drop zone covers the whole character, not just the small box they see, so a near miss still counts.

Show the reading order

Karaoke-style highlighting lights up each character in order, because my kid wasn’t sure which character to start reading from.

Let kids tap when speaking fails

Speech-to-text didn’t always catch what they said, so multiple choice sits on the same screen as a fallback.

To keep them learning

Make every fun part teach

Watching my kid use Khan Academy, I saw that when the games are separate from the learning, the time goes to the games. So every fun part of my app is part of the learning.

Reward progress

Rewards turned out to be extremely motivating: one of my kids did 38 short sessions, each one lesson on the path, in one sitting, just to earn the star and the treasure chest. What they can spend rewards on is still to come.

Don’t punish mistakes

I want my kids to keep trying, not feel discouraged. Losing hearts, as in Duolingo, is also hard for young kids to understand, and it can stop a lesson. I’d rather they take as many lessons as they want, so they can retry until they get it right.

Don’t block on a hard character

Some characters are just hard for them. Rather than forcing it, the app teaches other characters first and keeps reviewing the hard one.

For parents

Keep settings behind a parent gate

A quick sum question keeps kids out of settings. Behind it, parents can add a child, change the email and display name, and edit settings.

Behind the scenes

Two dictionaries, not one

Lessons have to work for any book, so characters live in their own dictionary, separate from any lesson plan. My agent argued for one table, with each row holding both the traditional and the simplified form. I argued it wouldn’t work. Simplified has fewer characters than traditional, so the mapping isn’t one-to-one: 髮 (hair) and 發 (to send out) both become 发, and 麵 (noodles) and 面 (face) both become 面. Pronunciation also differs by region: 髮 is fà in mainland China but fǎ in Taiwan.

Process

Proving it worked before making it fun

Step 1Wireframe

I built a bare-bones version first, to test whether the method worked with my kids and to catch bugs in the lesson logic before adding anything on top.

Step 2Static graphics

Once the concept worked, I styled it with static graphics. I designed the character with ChatGPT.

Step 3Animation

Then I added animation. I set up a tool where I pass in a static image of the character. It breaks the character into parts, analyzes how it looks from different angles, and produces an animation sequence from a prompt I write, built in Rive.

What I learned

What I learned from building it

What worked

Proving the method before building: paper flash cards first, then a bare wireframe that also caught bugs in the lesson logic, and only then the graphics and animation.

What surprised me

How much rewards motivate my kids. One of them did 38 sessions in one sitting, just to earn the star and the treasure chest.

What I learned about AI

AI’s capabilities keep changing. When I first tried, the agent wouldn’t animate in Rive at all. Set up as a dedicated tool, it could. Work I once had to watch closely now needs little supervision. So I make time to keep testing what it can newly do.

I first became curious about backgammon after hearing about it on a podcast, and built my own app to learn it.

AI built the first version quickly. Much of my design work was editing it: cutting what didn’t help, renaming what was unclear, and questioning rules that didn’t make sense. It has three difficulty levels and five ways to play, from a bot to Bluetooth.

Problem

Every app I tried was built around ads

Decisions

What I decided, and why

Free and ad-free, even if it earns less

Other apps are free but full of ads. I didn’t want to charge people or interrupt them, so playing is free and ad-free. The only purchases are optional boards.

Boards that feel like a real one

I own a handcrafted Persian backgammon board that’s beautiful to look at. I designed the boards with AI to bring that style into the app.

New players see the coach during onboarding. After that, it lives in Settings, because you only need it a few times and won’t once you know the game.

A game engine, for the first time

This was my first time using Flame, a Flutter game engine. It made the physics much more realistic: dice tumble and settle, and checkers lift, arc and land.
